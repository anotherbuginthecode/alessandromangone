## Adoption is a people problem before it is a tooling problem

Buying licenses is easy. Changing how a team works every day is not. I saw it in two very different rooms: client teams who had never written a prompt, and my own engineers, who knew the tools but still wrote every line by hand.

So the program ran on two tracks. One for client teams, to make generative AI part of their daily work. One for engineering, to make coding agents part of how we ship.

## Two audiences, one goal: change how the work gets done

On the client side, a single session could hold daily AI users, people who had tried it once and people worried about their jobs. On the engineering side, the team was delivering for enterprise clients, so adoption could not slow delivery down. We needed:

- A starting point measured team by team, not assumed.
- Plain-language explanations for non-technical people, and real depth for engineers.
- Exercises on real work: the documents, decks and codebases people touch every week.
- Clear rules on risk, data and review, so speed never came at the cost of quality.
- Material that outlives the sessions: prompts, templates, assistants and project scaffolding.

## For client teams: from assessment to roadmap

### Maturity assessment

A kick-off with the team lead on goals and data policy, then a survey on skills, tools and the tasks that take the most time. The results shape the training plan.

### AI literacy

Hands-on sessions that give everyone a shared vocabulary and a working method, practiced on the team's own material.

### Co-design

The team ranks the ideas collected during training by value and feasibility, describes the best ones on a use case canvas and prototypes them together.

### Roadmap

A closing presentation to management: priorities, owners and the KPIs that will track results.

## For engineers: from issue to merged PR

### Training that looks like real work

Training material, PRD templates and live demos on real projects, plus an internal course on agentic coding. The goal was not to try a tool, but to change the default way we build.

### A project starts with the board

I built the project templates, the CI/CD pipelines and the agent skills that bootstrap a new project together with an automated kanban board. From day one, coding agents can pick up issues from that board and work them on their own.

### Autonomy is a property of the issue

In my own workflow, and at Matcha, every issue is tagged with how much an agent can do alone. Well-scoped issues go straight to an agent, which delivers a tested pull request. Ambiguous ones stay with a person until they are clear enough to hand over.

### People own the review

Agents write code; people decide what ships. Every pull request goes through human review and merge, so accountability stays with the team.

## The same rule in every room: AI does the work, people own the outcome

Whether the audience was a marketing team or a group of senior engineers, the program ended in the same place: AI handles more of the work, and people stay responsible for what it produces. For client teams that meant a method and a roadmap. For my team it meant a new default, where the board feeds the agents and the review belongs to us.
